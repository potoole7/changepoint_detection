\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\textcolor{red}{From confirmation report}
We now consider settings in which the extremal dependence structure is non-stationary, and may change over time.
For example, meteorological variables observed across spatial locations may exhibit changes in extremal dependence due to shifts in climate regimes, while biomarker measurements collected longitudinally for individuals may display structural changes associated with disease progression, treatment effects, or lifestyle changes \citep{katz2010statistics, Gilles2018, panagoulias2025extreme, Talento2025}.
In such settings, it is of interest to detect changepoints or structural breaks in the extremal dependence structure.

In the existing framework, the multivariate ``groups'' could, in principle, represent different time periods.
However, this method was not developed to accommodate temporally evolving dependence structures within an already multivariate setting (e.g., spatial), and we assumed that the data within each group was stationary.
This is limiting in applications where tail dependence is expected to vary over time.
In environmental contexts, for example, climate change may strengthen or weaken extremal dependence across an entire spatial domain, as suggested by \citet{Zhong2022}, who found that recent Southern European heatwaves have been more widespread than in the past.
More generally, several types of structural change may occur. 
In some situations the extremal dependence structure may shift globally across all sites, corresponding to a regime change affecting the entire system. 
In other settings, only a subset of sites may change their extremal dependence behaviour, for example by switching between clusters of locations with similar tail dependence characteristics.
Discarding the stationarity assumption and allowing for such temporal changes in extremal dependence is therefore a natural and important extension of our current work.
It would enable us to identify time periods with distinct spatial patterns of tail dependence, as well as the specific changepoints at which these transitions occur.
Therefore, a key focus of this work will be the development of methods for changepoint detection in multivariate extremal dependence structures, building on the divergence-based framework introduced in our first paper. 

There has been some previous work on changepoint detection in multivariate dependence structures, whether in the context of extremes or more generally.
For example, \citet{McGonigle2025} developed a non-parametric sliding window approach for detecting changepoints in pairwise extremal dependence. 
Also, \citet{Alouni2026} developed a Bayesian clustering algorithm for multivariate extremes which utilises a mixture of Dirichlet distributions, and they show how it can be used to temporally cluster negative returns for several S\&P 500 stock sectors, thereby identifying periods of time with similar extremal dependence structures across the sectors.
In \citet{Agarwal2025}, a likelihood-based method for detecting temporal changepoints in spatio-temporal data is developed, and applied to daily wind speed data in Ireland, similar to the data we used in our first paper. 
We hope to build on these existing approaches and also the divergence-based clustering framework developed in our paper to develop a method for detecting changepoints in multivariate extremal dependence structures, which can be applied to a wide range of datasets and applications.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

Suppose we have $D$ spatial sites and observe $d$ variables across $T$ time points at each site. Our objective is to analyse the tail behaviour of $\boldsymbol{X}_s^{(t)}$, in particular the extremal dependence of the $d$ variables at the individual time points and sites, and to assess whether there is evidence of non-stationarity in the tail behaviour at one or more site. Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:cp} introduces our permutation-based procedure for detecting a structural breakpoint in tail behaviour using the pairwise similarities between fitted CE models.

\subsection{The conditional extremes model}
\label{subsec:ce}

Following \citet{Keef2013}, we transform each component of $\boldsymbol{X}_s^{(t)}$ to standard Laplace margins, yielding the random vector $\boldsymbol{Y}_s^{(t)} = (Y_{1,s}^{(t)}, \ldots, Y_{d,s}^{(t)})$. Formally, we achieve this transformation by defining
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,s}^{(t)} =
\begin{cases}
\log\left\{2 F_{i,s}^{(t)}\left(X_{i,s}^{(t)}\right)\right\} & \mbox{for}~F_{i,s}^{(t)}\left(X_{i,s}^{(t)}\right) < 0.5,\\
-\log\left\{2 \left[1-F_{i,s}^{(t)}\left(X_{i,s}^{(t)}\right)\right]\right\} & \mbox{for}~F_{i,s}^{(t)}\left(X_{i,s}^{(t)}\right) \geq 0.5,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:Laplace}
\end{equation}
where $F_{i,s}^{(t)}(\cdot)$ is the cumulative distribution function of $X_{i,s}^{(t)}$ ($i=1,\ldots,d;~s=1,\ldots,D;~t=1,\ldots,T$). Let $\boldsymbol{Y}^{(t)}_{-i,s}$ denote $\boldsymbol{Y}^{(t)}_s$ with its $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ component removed. The CE framework postulates that, for a sufficiently large threshold $u_i^{(t)}$, the conditional distribution of $\boldsymbol{Y}_{-i,s}^{(t)}$ given $Y_{i,s}^{(t)} = y$ can approximately be represented as 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ce_model_spat}
\bigl(\boldsymbol{Y}_{-i,s}^{(t)} \mid Y_{i,s}^{(t)} = y\bigr)
=
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)} y
+
y^{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}}
\boldsymbol{Z}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)},
\qquad y > u_{i,s}^{(t)},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}\in[-1,1]^{d-1}$, $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}\in(-\infty,1]^{d-1}$, and all vector operations are applied componentwise. Further, the residual vector $\boldsymbol{Z}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}$ is assumed to be independent of $Y_{i,s}^{(t)}$. The parameter vectors $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}$ govern, respectively, the strength of extremal dependence and the rate at which the conditional variability scales with the conditioning value.

For inference and to quantify similarity of CE models, we adopt the working assumption proposed by \citet{Heffernan2004} that
\[
\boldsymbol{Z}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}
\sim
\operatorname{MVN}_{d-1}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)},
\Sigma_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}
\right),
\]
which gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ce_conditional_gaussian}
\bigl(\boldsymbol{Y}_{-i,s}^{(t)} \mid Y_{i,s}^{(t)} = y\bigr)
\sim
\operatorname{MVN}_{d-1}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}_{\mid i,s}y
+
y^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}_{\mid i,s}}
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t)}_{\mid i,s},
\;
\Omega^{(t)}_{\mid i,s}(y)
\right),
\qquad y > u_{i,s}^{(t)},
\end{equation}
where
\[
\Omega^{(t)}_{\mid i,s}(y)
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
y^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}_{\mid i,s}}
\right)
\Sigma_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
y^{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mid i,s}^{(t)}}
\right).
\]
We fit this model separately for each conditioning variable and spatial location using maximum likelihood estimation, without imposing spatial smoothness. The threshold $u_{i,s}^{(t)}$ is fixed at a high quantile $u$ of the standard Laplace distribution, for all $i$ and $s$, to ensure that the resulting CE models are comparable across locations.

Although the Gaussian assumption~\eqref{eq:ce_conditional_gaussian} is known to suffer from parameter identifiability issues and may lead to invalid inference \citep{Keef2012_flooding}, the results of \cite{OToole2026clustering} suggest that these shortcomings have limited impact when the primary objective is to analyse spatial variation in extremal dependence among $\boldsymbol{X}_1^{(t)}, \ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_D^{(t)}$. It is therefore reasonable to expect that the assumption remains adequate when additionally allowing for non-stationarity in the extremal dependence structure. Moreover, the assumption yields substantial computational advantages, as discussed in the next subsection.

\subsection{A divergence measure for conditional extremes models}
\label{subsec:div}

\cite{OToole2026clustering} introduce the conditional extremal skew-geometric Jensen--Shannon divergence as a dissimilarity measure between CE models; the measure is based on the skew-geometric Jensen--Shannon divergence \citet{Nielsen2019}. Let $H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y)$ denote the distribution function of the CE model in \eqref{eq:ce_conditional_gaussian} for $\boldsymbol{Y}_{-i,s}^{(t)}\mid (Y_{i,s}^{(t)} = y)$, $y>u$. The skew-geometric Jensen--Shannon divergence of $H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y)$ and $H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jsga}
\JSGa{}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y) \Vert H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)\right]
=
\lambda
\KL\left\{
G_{\lambda}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y),H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)\right] \Vert H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y)
\right\}
+
(1-\lambda)
\KL\left\{
G_{\lambda}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y),H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)\right] \Vert H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)
\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\KL$ denotes the Kullback--Leibler divergence, $G_{\lambda}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y),H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)\right]$ is the distribution with density proportional to the weighted geometric mean of the densities corresponding to $H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y)$ and $H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)$, and $\lambda\in[0,1]$. We set $\lambda=0.5$, yielding a symmetric, non-negative measure of dissimilarity. Due to the Gaussian assumption~\eqref{eq:ce_conditional_gaussian}, the expression in~\eqref{eq:jsga} can be evaluated in closed form; see \citet{Nielsen2019} and \citet{OToole2026clustering} for details.

The quantity \eqref{eq:jsga} only compares the fitted CE models at the two locations for $Y_{i,s}^{(t)} = Y_{i,s'}^{(t)} = y$. We therefore take its expectation with respect to the conditioning variable. As all marginal distributions are standard Laplace and the conditioning threshold is fixed as $u$ across locations (see Section~\ref{subsec:ce}), the expected skew-geometric Jensen--Shannon divergence for two CE models is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jsga_integral}
E_{i,s,s'}^{(t)} = 
\mathbb{E}
\left\{
\JSGa{}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}\left(Y\right) \Vert H_{i,s'}^{(t)}\left(Y\right)\right]
\mid Y>u
\right\}
=
\int_{u}^{\infty}
\JSGa{}\left[H_{i,s}^{(t)}(y) \Vert H_{i,s'}^{(t)}(y)\right]
f(y-u) \,\mathrm{d}y,
\end{equation}
where $f(y-u) = \exp\{-(y-u)\}$ is the density of $Y_{i,s}\mid (Y_{i,s}>u)$ for $u>0$; we refer the reader to \cite{OToole2026clustering} for a theoretical treatment of this dissimilarity measure. In practice, we approximate the expectation using Monte Carlo integration and, to avoid unstable extrapolation beyond the observed range, we evaluate the integral only up to a suitably high upper quantile of the standard exponential distribution. By averaging $E_{i,s,s'}^{(t)}$ over the $d$ conditioning variables for each pair ($s,s'$) of sites, we obtain the dissimilarity matrix $M^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$, with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:distance_matrix}
M^{(t)} =\left\{M_{s,s'}^{(t)}\right\}_{s,s'=1,\ldots,D}, \quad  
M_{s,s'}^{(t)} = 
\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d E_{i,s,s'}^{(t)}
,\quad t=1,\ldots,T.
\end{equation}
Th resulting matrix forms the basis for the procedure described in the next subsection for analysing non-stationarity in the tail behaviour.

\subsection{Testing for non-stationarity in extremal dependence}
\label{subsec:cp}

Let $\tau\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$ be a time point and define the time blocks $\mathcal{T}_1(\tau) = \{\tau-n+1,\ldots,\tau\}$ and $\mathcal{T}_2(\tau) = \{\tau+1,\ldots,\tau+n\}$, where $n$ is a user-specified block length. To assess stationarity in the tail behaviour of  $\boldsymbol{Y}_1^{(t)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Y}_D^{(t)}$, we construct a statistical test based on the pairwise dissimilarities of CE models fitted using observations from the two time blocks. For block $\mathcal{T}_j(\tau)$~($j=1,2$), we assume that the parameters of the CE model in \eqref{eq:ce_conditional_gaussian} remain constant across time within the block. This yields a matrix $M^{(j)}(\tau)$, as defined in \eqref{eq:distance_matrix}, which summarises the pairwise dissimilarities between sites. Under stationarity, $M^{(1)}(\tau) = M^{(2)}(\tau)$, and this motivates the null hypothesis
\begin{equation}
H_0: M^{(1)}(\tau) = M^{(2)}(\tau),
\label{eq:H0}
\end{equation}
with the alternative hypothesis $H_1: M^{(1)}(\tau) \neq M^{(2)}(\tau)$.

Given the data for the time blocks $\mathcal{T}_1(\tau)$ and $\mathcal{T}_2(\tau)$, we obtain estimates $\hat{M}_1(\tau)$ and $\hat{M}^{(2)}(\tau)$ of $M^{(1)}(\tau)$ and $M^{(2)}(\tau)$, respectively, by first estimating the CE model parameters via maximum likelihood inference, followed by Monte Carlo integration for evaluating \eqref{eq:jsga_integral}. 
Two popular statistics are considered to test $H_0$ against $H_1$. 
The first is the Frobenius norm of the difference between $\hat{M}^{(1)}(\tau)$ and $\hat{M}^{(2)}(\tau)$: 
\begin{equation} 
\label{eq:frob}
N_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(F)}(\tau) \coloneq \left\| \hat{M}^{(1)}(\tau) - \hat{M}^{(2)}(\tau) \right\|_{\mathrm{F}} = \sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^D \sum_{s'=1}^D \left[\hat{M}^{(1)}_{s,s'}(\tau) - \hat{M}^{(2)}_{s,s'}(\tau)\right]^2}.
\end{equation}
The second statistic is the infinity, or maximum-entry, norm:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:infinity}
N_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(I)}(\tau) \coloneq \max_{s,s'=1,\ldots,D}\left|\hat{M}^{(1)}_{s,s'}(\tau) - \hat{M}^{(2)}_{s,s'}(\tau)\right|.
\end{equation}
The Frobenius norm~\eqref{eq:frob} captures the overall magnitude of change, whereas the infinity norm~\eqref{eq:infinity} is only sensitive to the largest pairwise difference. 
Consequently, their relative performance under non-stationarity is expected to depend on whether changes occur across many sites or are concentrated in a small number of locations. 
For notational brevity, we write $N_{\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)$ in the rest of the section when referring to $N_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(F)}(\tau)$ or $N_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(I)}(\tau)$.
We recognise that other statistics for the matrix difference could be considered, but we focus on these two for their popularity, simplicity and interpretability, as an illustration of the proposed permutation testing procedure.

To determine whether to reject $H_0$ based on $N_{\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)$, we implement a permutation test, which involves comparing the observed test statistic to a reference distribution generated by randomly permuting the data \citep{Good2004}. This approach avoids strong parametric assumptions about the sampling distribution of the dissimilarity matrices and is exact when the observations are exchangeable under $H_0$. The null distribution of $N_{\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)$ is approximated using $B$ permutations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.] 
  \item Generate $B$ random permutations of the $2n$ observations from the period $\mathcal{T}_1(\tau)\cup\mathcal{T}_2(\tau)$. For each permutation, assign the first $n$ observations to one block, and the remaining $n$ observations to the second block. 
  \item For each permutation $b\in\{1,\ldots,B\}$, estimate the CE model parameters and, subsequently, the matrix estimates, yielding estimates $\hat{M}^{(1)}_b(\tau)$ and $\hat{M}_b^{(2)}(\tau)$.
  \item Compute the test statistic $N_b$ as defined in \eqref{eq:frob} or \eqref{eq:infinity} using $\hat{M}^{(1)}_b(\tau)$ and $\hat{M}_b^{(2)}(\tau)$. 
\end{enumerate}
The empirical p-value for the test is then
\begin{equation} 
\label{eq:perm_pval}
\hat{p} =
\frac{ 1+ \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbf{1} \left\{ N_b \geq N_{\mathrm{obs}}(\tau) \right\} }{B+1},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ denotes the indicator function. A value of $\hat{p}$ below the significance level $\alpha$, $\hat{p}<\alpha$, provides evidence for a difference in the pairwise dissimilarity structure of the fitted CE models between the two blocks, suggesting non-stationarity in tail behaviour around time point $\tau$.  

When the number of values considered for $\tau$ is modest, as in Sections 3 and 4, permutation testing can be performed over the complete candidate range. In settings with longer time series or a substantially larger numbers of sites, one may first wish to identify local maxima, thereby reducing computational cost. Moreover, a suitable block size $n$ must be chosen to ensure reliable estimation of the CE model parameters within each time block, while also providing sufficient power to detect changes. To ensure comparability of results in Sections 3 and 4, we apply the same $n$ for each candidate $\tau$ and only consider time points with $\tau\in\{n,\ldots,T-n\}$. Finally, we note that the proposed procedure assesses non-stationarity locally around a given time point $\tau$. To test for non-stationarity across the entire observation period, one may either perform the test for each admissible value of $\tau$ and apply an appropriate multiple-testing correction, or define a global test statistic
$$
N_{\max} \coloneq \max_{\tau=n,\ldots,T-n} N_{obs}(\tau).
$$
A permutation test can then be constructed using the corresponding maximum statistic under each permutation. This automatically accounts for the multiple testing induced by considering multiple values of $\tau$.

\section{Simulation Study}
\label{sec:sim}

% We demonstrate the performance of our proposed changepoint detection procedure through a simulation study.
% To assess the ability of our method to detect changes in extremal dependence, we generate synthetic datasets with known changepoints designed to mimic the application to Spanish temperature and precipitation data in Section~\ref{sec:app}. \textcolor{red}{More emphasis on ``gradual'' change?}
We demonstrate the ability of the proposed procedure to detect temporal changes in multivariate extremal dependence through a simulation study. 
The simulation design broadly reflects the structure of the Spanish application in Section~\ref{sec:app}, with two variables observed weekly at multiple sites over a sequence of ``season-years'' (defined as the period from October to September).
Rather than imposing an instantaneous changepoint, we allow the extremal dependence structure to evolve gradually over a specified transition period, to better reflect the types of changes that may occur in environmental systems.
The objective is therefore not to recover a single exact change time, but to assess whether the procedure identifies evidence of a transition and whether the Frobenius and infinity norms respond differently to widespread and localised changes.

In Section~\ref{subsec:sim_setup}, we describe the simulation setup and the scenarios considered, which include a null scenario with no change, a local change affecting a subset of sites, and a global change affecting all sites.
Section~\ref{subsec:sim_results} presents the results of the simulation study, comparing the performance of the Frobenius and infinity norms in detecting changes under each scenario.

\subsection{Simulation setup}
\label{subsec:sim_setup}

We simulate two variables at $D=40$ sites over $60$ season-years, with $12$ weekly observations in each season-year. 
At each site and time point, the two variables are generated from a bivariate Student $t$-copula with three degrees of freedom \citep{Demarta2007-ap}. 
The sites are generated independently, and the weekly observations are conditionally independent given the site- and year-specific copula parameters. 
% Known generalised Pareto marginal distributions, with shape parameter $\xi=-0.05$, scale parameter $\sigma=1$ and threshold zero, are used for both variables. 
The observations are transformed to standard Laplace margins using their known marginal distributions.
Across all CE fits, the same threshold $u$, set to the $85$th quantile of the standard Laplace distribution, as well as $500$ Monte Carlo samples of the conditioning variable, are used to evaluate the expected divergence in Equation~\eqref{eq:jsga_integral}, to ensure comparability of the resulting conditional distributions.

The sites are divided into three baseline extremal dependence groups. 
Sites $1,\ldots,14$ have initial $t$-copula correlation parameter $\rho_{s,0}=0.1$, sites $15,\ldots,27$ have $\rho_{s,0}=0.5$, and sites $28,\ldots,40$ have $\rho_{s,0}=0.8$.

\label{eq:sim_rho}

\operatorname{atanh}(\rho_{s,0})
+
A_s\delta_z g(t)
\right\},
\end{equation}
where $A_s$ indicates whether site $s$ is affected by the change, $\delta_z$ controls its magnitude on the Fisher-$z$ scale, and $g(t)$ determines its temporal progression. \todo{Can increase g(t) to make temporal progression faster}
Specifically,
\begin{equation}

\begin{cases}
0, & t \leq t_0, \\
\dfrac{t - t_0}{t_1 - t_0}, & t_0 < t < t_1, \\

We consider three scenarios:

  \item \textbf{Null:} no sites are affected, so that $A_s=0$ for all $s$;

For each scenario, we generate $100$ independent datasets and apply the changepoint procedure described in Section~\ref{subsec:cp}. 
For the screening step, we fit using adjacent blocks of $15, 20, 25$ and $30$ season-years, and evaluate candidate split points over the complete feasible range.

\begin{itemize}
\item The infinity norm also exhibits increased power under the global alternative, but the improvement is less pronounced. 
\item At the 1995 candidate, its rejection rates are approximately 22\% at the 5\% level and 28\% at the 10\% level, compared with null rejection rates of approximately 9\% and 14\%, respectively.

\item The stronger performance of the Frobenius norm is consistent with the global nature of the alternative. 
\item As the dependence structure changes at every site, differences accumulate across many entries of the dissimilarity matrix and consequently contribute to the Frobenius norm. 
\item The infinity norm instead depends only on the largest entrywise difference.
\end{itemize}

% Conclusions
\begin{itemize}
\item The power profiles are neither sharply peaked nor strictly monotone.
\item This is consistent with the gradual nature of the simulated transition: there is no unique instantaneous changepoint, and nearby candidate splits compare temporal blocks containing different mixtures of pre-transition and transitional observations.

\item Even under the global alternative, the largest observed pointwise power is approximately 40\%. 
\item The procedure therefore has moderate rather than high power under this simulation design. 
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item Figure~\ref{fig:sim_local} compares the empirical null rejection rates with the pointwise power obtained when the gradual change is restricted to five of the 40 sites.
\item When the correlation parameter at the affected sites increases from 0.1 towards 0.3, the rejection profiles for both norms remain similar to those obtained under the null scenario. 
  This magnitude of local change is therefore not reliably detectable under the present simulation design.
\item Increasing the final correlation to 0.6 produces a clearer response for the Frobenius norm. 
  At the 10\% significance level, its rejection rate increases from approximately 16\% at the 1990 candidate to 23\% at 1995, compared with null rates of approximately 12\% and 13\%, respectively.
\item At the 5\% level, the rejection rate for the Frobenius norm reaches approximately 12\%--13\% for some of the later candidate splits, compared with approximately 5\%--8\% under the null. 
\item The improvement in power is therefore present but modest.

\item The Wilson intervals overlap substantially across the null and local-change scenarios. 
\item Consequently, the evidence for improved detection under the stronger local alternative is better characterised by the overall increase across the later candidate splits than by any individual candidate year.

\item The infinity norm shows little systematic improvement under either local alternative. 
\item Under the stronger change, its rejection rate at the 1995 candidate is approximately 9\% at the 5\% level and 17\% at the 10\% level, compared with null rates of approximately 9\% and 14\%, respectively.

\item Thus, contrary to the initial expectation, the infinity norm does not provide greater sensitivity to the localised change considered here. 
\item One possible explanation is that the gradual change does not produce an individual entrywise difference sufficiently large to dominate the estimation variability in the dissimilarity matrices, instead producing differences in entire rows and columns of the matrix, which is more effectively captured by the Frobenius norm.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item Overall, the procedure is reasonably well calibrated pointwise and has greater power against the global alternative than against either local alternative. 
\item Detectability depends on both the magnitude and spatial extent of the change, as well as on whether it produces an accumulated signal or a sufficiently large entrywise difference in the estimated dissimilarity matrix.
\end{itemize}

% Marginal preprocessing
\begin{itemize}
\item The conditional extremes framework is applied after transforming each variable to a common marginal distribution. 
\item Consequently, systematic changes in the marginal distributions over time could otherwise be confounded with changes in the extremal dependence structure.
\item Both variables exhibit seasonal variation, while weekly maximum temperature in particular also displays a long-term marginal trend. 
\item We therefore preprocess the marginal distributions before fitting the conditional extremes models.
\item At each station, and for each variable, we consider observations from each calendar month separately and fit a linear trend in year. 
\item The estimated trend is removed from the observations for that month. 
\item Treating months separately allows the magnitude of the temporal trend to vary throughout the year while accounting for the pronounced marginal seasonality.
\item This detrending removes an estimated linear change in the location of each monthly marginal distribution; it does not imply that all temporal dependence has been removed. 
\item In particular, some short-range dependence may remain between successive weekly observations, and consecutive values of the 13-week drought index are constructed from overlapping precipitation windows.
\item Following detrending, the observations are grouped by meteorological season. 
\item For each station and variable, the resulting seasonal observations are transformed standard Laplace margins using the empirical rank transform.
\item The conditional extremes and changepoint analyses are then performed separately for winter, spring, summer and autumn.
\end{itemize}

% Conditional extremes specification
\begin{itemize}
\item We fit the conditional extremes model of \citet{Heffernan2004} using a common dependence threshold equal to the theoretical 80th percentile of the standard Laplace distribution. 
\item The same threshold is used for both variables, at every station, in both temporal blocks and at every candidate changepoint. 
\item This ensures that exceedances have a common interpretation throughout the analysis.
\item The 80th-percentile threshold provided a satisfactory empirical compromise between restricting attention to the joint tail and retaining enough exceedances for stable model estimation. 
\item In particular, the resulting clustering solutions exhibited clear spatial contiguity. \textcolor{red}{Add a sensitivity analysis for the dependence threshold.}
\item The Monte Carlo approximation used to construct the pairwise dissimilarities employs the same sample from the standard Laplace distribution in every temporal comparison. 
\item Holding this sample fixed avoids introducing additional Monte Carlo variation between candidate changepoints.
\item The conditional fits from the two conditioning directions are combined into the aggregated dissimilarity matrix defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}. \textcolor{red}{Test for others}
\item All screening, testing and clustering calculations reported below use this matrix. \textcolor{red}{Confirm terminology and add sensitivity results for the alternative dissimilarity matrices, if required.}
\end{itemize}

% Screening and changepoint-test specification
\begin{itemize}
\item For the Spanish application, the permutation procedure is adapted to preserve the temporal and spatial structure within each season. 
\item Rather than permuting individual weekly observations, we permute complete season-years.
\item For spring, summer and autumn, the season-year is the calendar year in which the observations occur. 
\item Winter spans two calendar years; December is therefore assigned to the same season-year as the following January and February. 
% \item For example, December 1997 and January-February 1998 jointly constitute winter 1998.
\item At a candidate season-year $(t)$, the two temporal blocks consist of the (25) season-years ending at $(t)$ and the (25) season-years beginning at $(t+1)$, respectively. 
\item Thus, $(t)$ denotes a possible change between season-years $(t)$ and $(t+1)$.
\item In each pointwise permutation, the 50 season-years from the two blocks are pooled and randomly divided into two pseudo-blocks containing 25 season-years each. 
\item Every weekly observation belonging to a given season-year is moved as a single unit, and the same reassignment is applied simultaneously to both variables and all 40 stations. 
\item This preserves the dependence between observations within a season-year and the spatial correspondence between stations.
\item The validity of this permutation procedure relies on treating season-years as exchangeable under the null hypothesis of no change in the extremal dependence structure. 
\item Although permuting complete season-years preserves within-year dependence, it does not preserve any residual dependence between successive season-years.
\end{itemize}

% Choice of block length
\begin{itemize}
\item The choice of block length involves a bias-variance trade-off. 
\item Shorter blocks provide better temporal localisation but contain fewer threshold exceedances, resulting in unstable or failed conditional extremes fits. 
\item Longer blocks provide more stable estimates but leave fewer feasible candidate changepoints and smooth changes over a wider temporal interval.
\item We investigated block lengths of 15, 20, 25 and 30 season-years. 
\item With 15-year blocks, many candidate comparisons failed because at least one conditional fit contained too few exceedances. 
\item Twenty-year blocks were reliable for winter but produced substantial failure rates for several other seasons. 
\item All candidate comparisons fitted successfully with 25-year blocks. 
\item Increasing the length to 30 years also produced stable fits, but left only two to five candidate changepoints per season.
\item We therefore use 25 season-years on either side of each candidate throughout the primary analysis. 
\item This was the shortest common block length that produced stable fits across all seasons while retaining a useful range of candidate changepoints. \textcolor{red}{Present the alternative block lengths as a sensitivity analysis or in the supplementary material(?).}
\end{itemize}

% Spring
\begin{itemize}
  \item The full-record spring solution (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:app_clust_spring}) also exhibits substantial spatial coherence.
  \item Cluster 2 extends along much of the northern and northwestern coast, whereas clusters 1 and 3 occupy broader and partially overlapping interior, eastern and southern regions.
  \item Geography therefore provides a useful broad interpretation of the spring clusters, although several spatial outliers confirm that the clustering is determined by fitted extremal dependence rather than spatial proximity alone.
  \item The dissimilarity matrix for Spring (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:diss_spring}) is fairly well block-structured, with intra-cluster dissimilarities generally smaller than inter-cluster dissimilarities.
  \item As no consistent changepoint is identified for spring, this solution provides a useful stationary seasonal comparison with the temporally divided solutions for winter, summer and autumn.
\end{itemize}

% Summer
\begin{itemize}
  \item The two summer periods retain a common large-scale structure, although a number of stations change membership after 1990.
  \item During 1960--1990, cluster 1 is concentrated more strongly across northern and eastern Spain, while cluster 2 contains many southern and western stations. Cluster 3 occupies an intermediate and geographically mixed position.
  \item After 1990, several cluster-2 stations located away from the southern coast move to cluster 1, producing a somewhat clearer geographical division between northern/eastern and southern/western locations.
  \item The summer reorganisation is less extensive than that observed for winter and appears to be driven more strongly by a limited number of stations.
  \item As for winter, the post-1990 dissimilarity matrix (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:diss_summer}) is less clearly block-structured and has a lower overall dissimilarity scale than the earlier matrix.
  \item The elbow curves in Figure~\ref{fig:elbow} indicate that \(k=2\) is also a plausible representation of the summer structure. In the two-cluster solution, the members of the intermediate cluster are absorbed into cluster 2, indicating weaker separation between these groups than between either group and cluster 1.

  \item The signed difference matrix between the two summer periods (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sign_matrix_diff})
  % \[
  %     \Delta M^{(\mathrm{S})}
  %     =
  %     M^{(\mathrm{S})}_{1960\text{--}1990}
  %     -
  %     M^{(\mathrm{S})}_{1991\text{--}2020},
  % \]
  is predominantly positive, providing evidence of an overall reduction in pairwise dissimilarity after 1990.
  \item Summer contains the largest individual absolute matrix changes among the three divided seasons, with maximum differences of approximately \(0.10\). These changes are concentrated in several prominent rows and columns rather than being distributed uniformly.
  \item Donostia/San Sebastián-Igeldo and Granada Base Aérea display particularly pronounced positive bands, indicating that their fitted dependence structures became more similar to those at many other stations after 1990.
  \item Granada Base Aérea also moves from cluster 2 to cluster 1. This transition is consistent with a weakening of the earlier distinction between its original cluster and the remaining dependence regimes.
  \item The concentration of the summer changes in a limited number of rows and columns is consistent with the comparatively strong changepoint evidence under the Infinity norm.
  \item Collectively, the summer results suggest partial convergence of the fitted dependence structures while retaining a broad north/east versus south/west spatial organisation.
\end{itemize}

% Autumn
\begin{itemize}
    \item The 1960--1986 autumn clustering resembles the spring solution in retaining a recognisable geographical structure, with distinct groups across western, central and eastern parts of the study region.
    \item The geographical separation becomes weaker during 1987--2020, with several northern, northeastern, southwestern and Mediterranean stations changing membership.
    \item This reorganisation is also reflected in the dissimilarity matrices (Supplementary Figure~\ref{diss_autumn}), with the later matrix appearing less distinctly block-structured.

    \item The autumn signed difference matrix is predominantly positive, indicating that many station pairs have smaller fitted dissimilarities after 1986.
    \item In contrast to summer, the positive autumn differences are distributed relatively widely throughout the matrix, rather than being dominated by only one or two stations.
    \item Autumn therefore provides evidence of a broad but generally moderate convergence in fitted extremal dependence structure.

    % \item Before 1987, the clusters are clearly differentiated by the upper DPI quantiles and by \(\widehat{\chi}(0.9)\) and \(\widehat{\bar{\chi}}(0.9)\). Some of this separation persists afterwards, although the marginal DPI differences weaken.
    % \item Stations that change cluster tend to have weaker empirical tail dependence before 1987 but stronger tail dependence afterwards. This apparent reversal provides a possible dependence-based explanation for the observed reassignment, although paired differences should be examined before drawing a firm conclusion.
\end{itemize}


% Overall interpretation
\begin{itemize}
    \item The three seasons with candidate temporal changes exhibit appreciable reorganisation of their estimated spatial clusters, but the form of this reorganisation differs between seasons.
    \item Winter exhibits the broadest redistribution of cluster membership and a mixture of increasing and decreasing pairwise dissimilarities.
    \item Summer retains its principal large-scale spatial structure, but contains several large station-specific changes that weaken the separation between some clusters.
    \item Autumn exhibits a more widespread reduction in pairwise dissimilarity, although its changepoint evidence is weaker and should be regarded as exploratory.

    \item Across the divided seasons, the later-period dissimilarity matrices generally exhibit weaker block structure and smaller pairwise dissimilarities. This indicates that the fitted conditional extremal dependence structures became less strongly differentiated across Spain.

    % \item This convergence does not by itself imply that extremal temperature--DPI dependence strengthened. Establishing the direction of dependence change requires paired station-level comparisons of \(\widehat{\chi}(0.9)\), \(\widehat{\bar{\chi}}(0.9)\), or fitted conditional extremes parameters.

    % \item The results likewise do not establish that anthropogenic climate change caused the observed reorganisation, nor that desertification increased. Such conclusions would require separate analysis of marginal drought severity, temporal trends, physical mechanisms and potential confounding factors.

    \item \textcolor{red}{Below two lines should possibly should go in discussion}
    \item These results suggest that the regional differentiation of compound temperature--DPI extremes appears to have weakened in the later periods, with locations displaying more similar fitted dependence structures despite retaining some broad geographical organisation.
    \item One must also be cautious that the periods used to estimate each clustering solution differ, particularly in the case of Winter 1999-2020, providing fewer observations for fitting the conditional extremes models. The increase in uncertainty inherent in using these shorter periods may effect our clustering. 
\end{itemize}
